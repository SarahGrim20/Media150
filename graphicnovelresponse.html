Reading Response

The storys choice to present itself as an interactive graphic novel initially feels almost disarming. The illustrated format, with its stylized drawings and clickable pathways, resembles something we might associate with fiction or even entertainment. But that expectation is quickly unsettled by the content: the lives of four women in Sierra Leone whose stories are marked by violence, loss, and survival. The contrast between form and subject becomes one of the most powerful aspects of the piece. Instead of distancing the reader, the graphic novel style draws you in gently, then confronts you with realities that are anything but soft.

The interactivity plays a crucial role in this experience. By requiring the reader to click through each womans story, the piece creates a sense of participation that mirrors, in a small way, the act of witnessing. You are not passively scrolling; you are choosing to continue, to see what happens next, to stay with each narrative. This structure slows down consumption and makes each story feel intentional. It also prevents the stories from blending together. Each womans experience, whether it is surviving a fistula during pregnancy, enduring rape and forced childbirth, losing multiple children, or being imprisoned for defending herself against an abusive husband remains distinct and deeply personal.

What makes the project especially striking is its integration of real video footage within the illustrated world. The drawings establish a certain visual language, but the sudden appearance of real nurses, counselors, and social workers disrupts that boundary. It is a reminder that these are not fictional scenarios but ongoing realities. The videos ground the narrative in documentary truth, reinforcing the credibility of the stories while also expanding their emotional impact. Seeing real people who are actively working to support these women adds a layer of immediacy and urgency that illustrations alone could not achieve.

At the same time, the content of the stories reveals a pattern of systemic injustice. All four women face not only individual hardship but also structural barriers rooted in a patriarchal, male-centered society. Their suffering is not random; it is shaped by cultural norms that limit womens autonomy, restrict access to healthcare, and normalize violence against them. Economic barriers further compound these issues, making it difficult or nearly impossible for them to seek treatment. Even when medical care exists, fear, such as the fear of Ebola, becomes another obstacle, illustrating how public health crises disproportionately affect those who are already vulnerable.

The closure of counseling and treatment centers during COVID-19 adds another devastating layer. It suggests that even the limited support systems available to these women are fragile and easily disrupted. The pandemic, much like Ebola before it, exposes and deepens existing inequalities rather than creating entirely new ones.

Ultimately, the interactive graphic novel format does more than tell these stories, it shapes how we engage with them. By combining illustration, interactivity, and real video, the piece creates a hybrid form that is both immersive and grounded. It challenges the reader to confront difficult realities while also reconsidering how stories like these are typically presented. Instead of statistics or distant reporting, we are given something more intimate, more participatory, and, as a result, more difficult to ignore.
