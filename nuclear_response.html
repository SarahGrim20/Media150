Nuclear Threat Reading Response

The New York Times article about preventing nuclear war left me feeling scared because it forced me to think about how fragile the future really is. It made me realize that there is no guarantee myself or any other students will be able to continue learning, living meaningful lives, or building their careers. The article emphasizes how close the world has come to nuclear disaster in the past and how current political tensions are making that possibility more realistic again. Reading it made me think about how many ordinary people are trying to study, create art, fall in love, and build meaningful lives while political leaders continue escalating conflict and prioritizing power over human safety.

One of the most frightening aspects of the article is the way it discussed modern political leaders contributing to instability. Donald Trump, for example, has repeatedly threatened other countries, discussed pulling out of NATO, and weakened support for Ukraine and President Volodymyr Zelensky. In my opinion, this makes nuclear conflict more likely because alliances between countries partly exist to prevent war. He also tries to appease other authoritarian leaders which leads to their countries becoming more powerful and more threatening to us. When leaders act impulsively or treat international politics like a competition for dominance, ordinary people are the ones who ultimately suffer the consequences.

As someone interested in art, journalism, and media studies, I also thought about how technology-based art could become a powerful tool for confronting these dangers.. Statistics about nuclear war can feel distant or abstract, but immersive digital art could force people to emotionally experience the reality of what is at stake. I was reminded of the work of guest speaker Nancy Baker Cahill, whose technologically driven art installations create emotionally overwhelming experiences that blur the line between physical and digital space.

One possible project could be a virtual reality artwork that simulates the aftermath of a nuclear bomb in a modern city. Participants could access the experience through their phones or VR headsets, making the piece participatory because viewers would actively move through the destroyed environment themselves instead of passively observing it. They would hear emergency broadcasts, see abandoned schools and homes, and witness the emotional devastation left behind. I think this kind of project would have a stronger emotional impact on people than simply reading facts because it would create empathy and fear through immersion.

Another possible artwork could involve a massive room filled with screens playing speeches from dictators and authoritarian leaders throughout history alongside modern political rhetoric. This installation would have a spatial affordance because viewers would physically navigate through the room while becoming overwhelmed by the gravity and intensity of the project. The experience of hearing conflicting speeches echo simultaneously would communicate how propaganda, nationalism, and fear repeat themselves across generations. Instead of learning about authoritarianism in a detached academic way, people would physically feel consumed by it.
